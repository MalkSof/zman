What an interruption really costs: 23 minutes, and other numbers

November 24, 2026

Everyone feels that interruptions are expensive. A few researchers have actually measured it, and the numbers are worse than most people guess. Here are the ones worth knowing, with where they come from, and a way to get your own.

23 minutes to get back

23 min 15 sAverage time to return to an interrupted task, in a field study of 36 information workers followed for three days.

The figure comes from Gloria Mark, professor of informatics at the University of California, Irvine, who shadowed managers, analysts, developers and engineers at their desks. Two details usually get lost when the number is quoted:

  • People did come back: 81.9% of interrupted work was resumed the same day.
  • They did not come back directly. On average there were about two other tasks in between. The cost is not one interruption, it is the chain it starts.

47 seconds on a screen

47 secondsAverage time people keep their attention on one screen before switching, in studies from 2016 to 2020.

Mark's first measurement, from a study run in 2003, was two and a half minutes. Less than twenty years later it was under one. This is not a measure of what the brain can do; it is a measure of what we actually do in front of a computer full of tabs and notifications.

An interruption every 2 minutes

Every 2 minutesHow often Microsoft 365 users are interrupted by a meeting, an email or a notification during the workday.

That is from Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index report on the "infinite workday", based on usage data from its own products. The same report found that 40% of people who are online at 6 a.m. are already checking email.

What the numbers add up to

Put together, they describe a day where focused work is the exception. If getting back takes over twenty minutes and something interrupts every two, long stretches of concentration do not happen by accident. They have to be protected.

They also explain why our own estimates are so bad. A day of constant switching feels busy, and busy feels productive. The only way to know is to measure.

How to measure your own day

  1. Track automatically. Timers you start by hand measure what you remember to time. An automatic tracker records which app and site was in front of you, all day.
  2. Look at focus blocks, not totals. Eight hours "in the editor" made of three-minute pieces is not eight hours of work. Count the stretches of 25 minutes or more.
  3. Find what breaks them. It is usually two or three sites or apps. Those are the ones to limit.
  4. Block them while you work. A 50-minute focus session with the usual suspects blocked is the cheapest fix there is.
